## What counts as a plumbing emergency versus something that can wait?

A true plumbing emergency is active water loss, sewage backup, or a gas smell, anything that gets worse by the hour if nobody shows up. A slow drip under the bathroom sink or a toilet that runs occasionally is a next-business-day call, not a 2 a.m. one.

Things that need someone tonight: a burst or actively leaking supply line, a sewage backup coming up through a floor drain or shower, a water heater leaking from the tank itself (not just a fitting), a sump pump that's failed during a storm, or water pooling around a slab area with no obvious source (a classic sign of a [slab leak](/blog/signs-of-a-slab-leak/)).

If you smell gas anywhere in the house, this is different from every other item on this list. Leave the building immediately. Don't flip light switches, don't use your phone inside, don't try to find the leak yourself. Once you're outside, call 911 or PG&E's emergency line. Only after the utility has confirmed the scene is safe should you call a plumber for the actual repair or pressure test. ## What should you do in the first 10 minutes of a plumbing emergency?

Shut off the water at the nearest fixture valve first, then the main if the leak is bigger than one fixture can isolate. Most Bakersfield homes have the main shutoff near the street-side hose bib or in the garage; know where yours is before you need it.

After the water's off: unplug nearby electronics, move anything valuable off a wet floor, and take photos of the standing water and any damaged drywall or flooring for your insurance file. If the water heater itself is leaking, shut off its cold-water supply valve and, for gas units, turn the gas control knob to pilot rather than trying to shut the gas off at the meter yourself.

If water already soaked carpet, baseboards, or drywall before you got the valve closed, that's a second problem beyond the pipe itself. ## What will an emergency plumber actually do when they arrive?

An emergency plumber isolates the problem first, then diagnoses the cause, then gives you a written price before touching anything beyond the immediate stop-the-damage step. If the fix is a simple supply line or fitting, expect it done the same visit. If it's a bigger job, slab leak, sewer line collapse, full repipe section, expect a same-visit diagnosis and a scheduled follow-up for the full repair.

For a burst or corroded section, the technician cuts out the damaged pipe and repairs it with a press fitting or soldered joint, matching the existing material where it makes sense. For a water heater failure, they'll check the tank itself versus a fitting or the TPR valve before recommending repair over replacement. Either way, you should get the diagnosis and the price in writing before work starts, not an invoice after the fact.
