## What should you check before calling for 24 hour AC repair?

Four checks rule out the most common no-cool causes in under five minutes: the breaker, the thermostat, the air filter, and the condensate safety switch. Running through them first can save you an after-hours trip charge if the fix is something you can do yourself.

Start at the electrical panel. A tripped double-pole breaker (usually labeled "AC" or "condenser") is one of the most common reasons a unit goes silent mid-cycle, especially during a string of 100-plus-degree days when the compressor is working hardest. Flip it fully off, then back on, once. If it trips again immediately, stop and call a professional; a breaker that won't hold usually means an electrical fault, not something to force.

Next, check the thermostat. Dead batteries or a blank screen will shut a system down completely even though nothing mechanical is wrong. Confirm it's set to "Cool" and the set point is below the room temperature. Then pull the air filter. A filter clogged with Kern County dust and pollen restricts airflow enough that the indoor coil can freeze, which often shows up as warm air or a system that short-cycles. For deeper troubleshooting on a unit that runs but doesn't cool, see [Air Conditioner Not Cooling: 9 Reasons Your AC Runs But the House Stays Hot](/blog/air-conditioner-not-cooling/). If the system won't power on at all, [Why Is My AC Not Turning On?](/blog/why-is-my-ac-not-turning-on/) walks through the same breaker-thermostat-filter sequence in more detail.

Last, look for a condensate safety switch. Many Bakersfield systems, especially anything installed or serviced in the last decade, have a float switch on the drain pan or line that shuts the whole unit down if condensate backs up. If that float is tripped, the fix is clearing the drain line, not restarting the compressor repeatedly.

## How do you keep the house safe for kids, seniors and pets until the tech arrives?

Move everyone to the coolest room in the house, usually a north-facing bedroom or a room without afternoon sun exposure, and use fans to keep air moving across skin rather than just circulating hot air. Heat builds fastest for infants, older adults, and pets, so don't wait for discomfort to become a warning sign.

Open windows at night if it's cooler outside than in, and close blinds on any windows that will catch morning sun before you're back up and running. A battery-powered fan or a car with air conditioning parked in the driveway is a reasonable stopgap for an hour or two if indoor temperatures climb into the 90s before help arrives. If anyone in the house shows signs of heat exhaustion, dizziness, heavy sweating, confusion, treat that as a medical situation first and the AC as a secondary problem.

## Is this a repair or is it time to replace the unit?

A repair usually makes sense when the system is under 10 years old, the fix addresses a single component (capacitor, contactor, fan motor), and the cost stays well under what a new system would run. Replacement starts looking better once a unit is pushing 15-plus years old, has needed more than one major repair in the last two summers, or still runs on an older, harder-to-source refrigerant.

A tech who pulls gauges and actually diagnoses the fault, rather than guessing, can usually tell you which category you're in on the spot.
