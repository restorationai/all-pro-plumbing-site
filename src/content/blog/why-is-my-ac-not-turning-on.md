**TL;DR:** When your AC won't turn on, start with four homeowner-safe checks: verify the thermostat is set to COOL with the temperature below room temp, check the breaker panel for a tripped breaker, look for a blinking light on the air handler (condensate float switch), and confirm the outdoor disconnect hasn't been pulled. If all four check out, the most likely culprit is a failed capacitor or a refrigerant issue, both of which require a licensed technician. Do not attempt to open the electrical panel on the condenser or touch internal components.

Your thermostat just clicked, the fan kicked on, and then... nothing. The outdoor unit is silent. Or maybe nothing happened at all when you dropped the set point. Before you assume the worst, there are four checks any homeowner can run safely in about ten minutes. Most no-start calls turn out to be one of these. The ones that aren't are still diagnosable, but they need a technician with the right tools.

## What are the homeowner-safe checks to run first?

Four checks cover the majority of no-start situations you can safely address yourself: thermostat settings, the breaker, the condensate float switch, and the outdoor disconnect. Run them in this order before calling anyone.

**1. Thermostat mode and batteries**

This sounds obvious, but it causes a surprising number of service calls. Make sure the thermostat is set to COOL (not FAN ONLY, not HEAT, not AUTO without a call for cooling). Set the target temperature at least 3-5 degrees below the current room temperature. If the display is dim or blank, replace the batteries even if you think they're fine. A low-battery thermostat will click and appear to send a signal without actually doing it.

If you have a smart thermostat, check whether it lost its Wi-Fi connection or whether a recent firmware update reset a schedule. Pull up the app and confirm the thermostat is actually calling for cooling, not just displaying the set point.

**2. The breaker panel**

AC systems use two breakers: one for the air handler (usually a 15-20 amp single-pole breaker) and one for the outdoor condenser (usually a 30-60 amp double-pole breaker). A tripped breaker sits in a middle position, not fully ON and not fully OFF. Push it fully to OFF first, then firmly back to ON. If it trips again immediately, stop. A breaker that won't hold is telling you there is a fault downstream, and forcing it is a fire risk. Call a technician.

If the breaker holds, wait two to three minutes before trying the thermostat again. The system's control board needs a moment to reset.

**3. The condensate float switch**

This is the one most homeowners don't know about. Your air handler has a condensate drain pan that collects the water pulled out of the air during cooling. When that pan fills up (usually because the drain line is clogged), a small float switch trips and shuts the whole system down to prevent a water overflow onto your ceiling or floor. The system won't start again until the pan is clear.

Look for a blinking light on the air handler, or a small white or red float device in the drain pan. If the pan has standing water in it, the drain line is clogged. You can try pouring a cup of distilled white vinegar down the condensate drain access port (a PVC tee with a cap, usually near the air handler) to break up algae buildup. Let it sit 30 minutes, then flush with water. If the pan drains and the float resets, the system should start. If the pan stays full or the line won't clear, a technician can flush it with a wet-vac or nitrogen purge.

**4. The outdoor disconnect**

Next to the outdoor condenser, there is a gray metal box mounted on the wall. It holds a disconnect block or fuse block that can be pulled to cut power to the unit for service. Sometimes these get pulled during a previous service call and not fully reseated, or a breeze can rattle a loose block. Open the box and confirm the disconnect block is fully seated. Some disconnects use cartridge fuses. If you have a non-contact voltage tester, you can check whether power is reaching the disconnect, but do not open the condenser cabinet itself.

## What if the fan runs but the outdoor unit doesn't start?

If the indoor air handler fan runs and blows air but the outdoor condenser never kicks on, the most common cause is a failed run capacitor. The capacitor is a cylindrical component inside the condenser cabinet that gives the compressor and fan motor the electrical jolt they need to start. When it fails, you will often hear a hum from the outdoor unit, and the fan blade may try to spin but can't get going on its own.

Capacitors store a significant electrical charge even after power is disconnected. Do not open the condenser cabinet and do not touch any internal components. This is a technician-only repair. A capacitor replacement typically takes 30-60 minutes and is one of the more affordable AC repairs, often in the $150-$350 range depending on the unit and capacitor rating, but the diagnosis has to be confirmed with a meter first.

Capacitor failure is especially common after a stretch of extreme heat. When outdoor temperatures stay above 100 degrees for days at a time, capacitors that were already weakening get pushed over the edge. If your system made it through summer and is now struggling to start on a hot afternoon, a capacitor is the first thing a technician will check.

## What about the thermostat clicking but the AC not responding at all?

If the thermostat clicks or the display shows a call for cooling but nothing happens anywhere in the system, the issue is likely in the control circuit rather than the high-voltage side. Possible causes include a blown low-voltage fuse on the air handler control board (usually a 3-5 amp automotive-style fuse), a failed contactor in the outdoor unit, or a wiring fault. Some systems also have a high-pressure or low-pressure safety switch that locks out the system when refrigerant pressure is out of range.

A low refrigerant charge is another technician-only diagnosis. Refrigerant doesn't "run out" on its own. If the system is low, there is a leak somewhere. Per [EPA Section 608 regulations](https://www.epa.gov/section608), refrigerant must be handled by a certified technician. Adding refrigerant without finding and repairing the leak just delays the next failure.

## What should you do if none of the checks fix it?

If you've worked through all four homeowner checks and the system still won't start, the problem is inside the electrical or refrigerant circuit and needs a licensed HVAC technician. At that point, the diagnostic visit is the right next step. A technician will use a multimeter to test the capacitor, contactor, control board, and safety switches, and a manifold gauge set to check refrigerant pressures.

When you call, tell them: whether the outdoor unit is completely silent or humming, whether the air handler fan runs, and which of the four checks you already ran. That information cuts the diagnostic time significantly.

If you're weighing whether a repair makes sense given the age of your system, the [DOE's guidance on central air conditioning](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/central-air-conditioning) recommends replacing systems older than 10-15 years when repair costs are significant, especially if the unit uses R-22 refrigerant (phased out as of 2020).
