**TL;DR:** When your AC won't turn on, start with four homeowner-safe checks before calling anyone: verify the thermostat is set to COOL with the temperature below room temp, reset any tripped breaker at the panel, look for a tripped condensate float switch near the air handler, and confirm the outdoor disconnect hasn't been pulled. If all four check out and the unit still won't start, the most likely culprit is a failed run capacitor, a refrigerant issue, or a bad contactor. Those require a licensed HVAC technician.

You set the thermostat to 72, hear a click, and then nothing. No fan spin outside, no airflow from the vents, just silence and a house that keeps climbing toward 90 degrees. Before you spend an hour on hold with an HVAC company, there are four checks you can run right now that cost nothing and fix the problem more often than you'd expect. Here's the order to run them, followed by the causes that genuinely need a technician.

## Why won't my AC turn on even though the thermostat is set to cool?

The most common reason an AC won't respond to a thermostat call is a settings error or dead batteries, not a broken system. Check three things at the thermostat before anything else: (1) the mode is set to COOL, not FAN ONLY or HEAT, (2) the set temperature is at least 3 degrees below the current room temperature, and (3) if the thermostat has batteries, swap them for fresh ones even if the screen looks fine. A low-battery thermostat will display normally but fail to send a signal to the air handler.

If your thermostat is wired (no batteries) and the screen is blank, check whether the air handler's door panel is fully seated. Most air handlers have a safety interlock switch behind the blower door. If the panel is even slightly ajar, the switch cuts power to the whole system. Press the door firmly until it clicks.

One more thermostat note: after a power outage, some programmable and smart thermostats reset to a default mode that isn't COOL. A quick mode check takes ten seconds and rules out the most embarrassing service call.

## Is a tripped breaker why my AC stopped working?

A tripped breaker is the second most common no-start cause, and it's free to fix yourself. Go to your electrical panel and look for two breakers labeled AC, AIR HANDLER, CONDENSER, or HVAC. A tripped breaker sits in the middle position, not fully ON or fully OFF. To reset it, push it firmly to OFF first, then back to ON. You should feel a click.

If the breaker resets and the AC runs normally, you're done. If it trips again within a few minutes, stop there. A breaker that keeps tripping is telling you something is drawing too much current, and running the system repeatedly will damage the compressor. Call a technician at that point.

One thing worth knowing: the outdoor condenser and the indoor air handler are usually on separate breakers. Check both. It's possible for the air handler to run (you hear the indoor fan) while the condenser breaker is tripped, which is why the fan works but no cold air comes out.

## What is a condensate float switch and how does it shut off my AC?

A condensate float switch is a small safety device mounted on the drain pan or condensate line beneath your air handler. When the AC cools air, it also pulls humidity out of it, and that water drains away through a condensate line. If that line clogs, the pan fills up. The float switch detects the rising water and cuts power to the system before the pan overflows onto your ceiling or floor.

If your AC was running fine and then suddenly stopped on a humid day, a clogged condensate line is a strong candidate. Look for a small white or gray switch near the base of the air handler, often with a single wire running to it. If the float is triggered, you'll usually see standing water in the drain pan.

The fix is clearing the clog. A wet/dry shop vac held over the outdoor condensate drain outlet for 30-60 seconds pulls most soft clogs free. Once the pan drains and the float drops back down, the system should restart. If the line clogs repeatedly, a [drain cleaning](/services/drain-cleaning/) inspection can clear the buildup and flush the line properly.

## How do I check the outdoor disconnect box?

Every central AC system has a disconnect box mounted on the exterior wall near the condenser, usually within a few feet of the unit. It's a gray or black weatherproof box, roughly the size of a thick paperback book. Open the cover and look inside. Some disconnects use a pull-out block that holds cartridge fuses; others have a simple switch or breaker.

If the pull-out block is missing or the switch is in the OFF position, that's your problem. Fused disconnects can also fail when one or both cartridge fuses blow. You can visually inspect glass-bodied fuses for a broken filament, but testing them with a multimeter is more reliable. If you're comfortable doing that, great. If not, this is a quick technician check.

Never reach into the disconnect box beyond what's visible from the outside. The line-side terminals inside carry 240V even when the disconnect is open.

## The fan hums but the condenser won't spin: what does that mean?

If you hear a humming sound from the outdoor unit but the fan blade isn't spinning, the run capacitor has almost certainly failed. The run capacitor is a cylindrical component inside the condenser's electrical compartment that provides the starting torque for the compressor and fan motor. When it fails, the motors receive power but can't start under load, which is why you hear the hum.

Capacitor failure is the single most common no-start in summer heat, and it's more common than most homeowners realize. The heat stress that capacitors endure during a 100-plus-degree stretch accelerates their failure rate significantly. The [U.S. Department of Energy notes](https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/central-air-conditioning) that central AC systems require periodic maintenance precisely because heat and electrical stress degrade components over time.

**Do not attempt to replace or touch the capacitor yourself.** Capacitors store an electrical charge even after the power is disconnected. A failed capacitor can still hold enough voltage to cause a serious shock. This is a technician-only repair. The good news: capacitor replacement is typically a straightforward service call, and the part itself is inexpensive. If your system is otherwise in good shape, this repair makes clear financial sense.

## What other technician-only causes could stop my AC from starting?

Beyond the capacitor, a few other causes require licensed hands:

**Failed contactor.** The contactor is an electrical relay that sends power to the compressor and fan when the thermostat calls for cooling. Contactors wear out, and their contact points can fuse or burn. Symptom: the thermostat clicks, the air handler runs, but the outdoor unit does nothing at all.

**Low refrigerant.** If refrigerant has leaked to a low enough level, the system's low-pressure safety switch will prevent the compressor from starting to avoid damage. You won't hear a hum. The system simply won't respond. Refrigerant work requires EPA Section 608 certification, so this is always a technician call.

**Failed compressor.** A compressor that has seized or shorted won't start regardless of what else is working. This is the most expensive repair in an AC system, and on older equipment it often tips the math toward replacement. If your system is more than 12-15 years old and the compressor has failed, it's worth getting a replacement quote alongside the repair estimate. Our [AC installation page](/services/ac-installation/) covers what a new system involves if that conversation comes up.

**Control board failure.** Modern systems use a circuit board in the air handler to sequence the startup. A failed board can prevent the system from responding to the thermostat at all, even when every other component is fine. Diagnosis requires reading fault codes or testing voltage at specific terminals.

## What should I do if none of my checks fixed it?

If you've confirmed the thermostat settings, reset the breakers, cleared the condensate line, and checked the outdoor disconnect, and the system still won't start, the problem is inside the electrical or mechanical components of the system. At that point, the next step is a diagnostic service call.

A technician will check the capacitor, contactor, refrigerant pressure, and control board in sequence. Most of these checks take 20-30 minutes with the right tools. If you've already run the four homeowner checks above, tell the technician when you call. It helps them arrive with the right parts and cuts the diagnostic time.
